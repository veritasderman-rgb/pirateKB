# FACTS ON LEGALIZATION OF RECREATIONAL MARIJUANA

LEGALIZATION OF RECREATIONAL MARIJUANA: THE FACTS

Uruguay was the first country to legalize recreational use of marijuana in 2013. Their minimum legal purchase age is 18 (MLPA-18). On October 17, 2018, the recreational use and personal cultivation of cannabis (marijuana) became legal in Canada (the MLPA is 18 in most provinces and 19 in a few). Numerous countries have decriminalized the possession of small amounts of marijuana, e.g., Austria, Belgium, Croatia, Czech Republic, Estonia, Italy, Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Slovenia, Spain, Switzerland, Ukraine, and many others.

Since 2013 in the United States (U.S.), Colorado, Washington, Alaska, Oregon, California, Maine, Massachusetts, Nevada, and Vermont have legalized the commercial production, distribution and possession of marijuana for non-medical purposes for adults aged 21 and older. Michigan will follow in 2019. Marijuana is legal for recreational use in the District of Columbia (DC), but not for production, distribution or selling. So 10 States plus DC have legalized the recreational use of marijuana.

Slightly over half of U.S. adults believe recreational marijuana should be legalized. Close to 22 million Americans use marijuana (self-report). Concerning youth, in 2014, 6.5% of 8th graders, 16.6% of 10th graders and 21.2% of 12th graders used marijuana in the past 30 days.

Some of the signs of marijuana use are bloodshot eyes, increased heart rate, sleepiness, poor coordination, delayed reaction time and an increased appetite. Some of the effects of marijuana on driving performance include a slow reaction time and ability to make decisions, impaired coordination, a distorted perception, and difficulty in problem-solving. Marijuana affects areas of the brain that control body movements, balance, memory and judgement [Sources: Lenne et al., 2010; Hartman & Huestis, 2012; Hartman, et al., 2015]

In driving simulators, marijuana dosed subjects had lane deviations, reduced mean speeds and increased following distance which suggests awareness and compensation. But this does not preclude control decrements. Whereas alcohol dosed subjects drove at higher mean speeds and spent a greater percent of time driving above the speed limit which suggests the risk-taking effects of alcohol. [Source: Hartman & Huestis, 2013].

In one study in the U.S., the odds of being in a crash for drivers with THC (marijuana) in their systems was 1.05 (adjusted for age & gender) compared to drivers with no THC. Keep in mind, two-thirds of the crashes were property damage only. On the other hand, the odds of being in a crash for a driver with a BAC = .05 was 2.07 (adjusted for age & gender and statistically significant)) compared to drivers with a BAC = .00. [Source: NHTSA, Compton & Berning (2015), DOT HS 812-117]. However, in studies in Europe, marijuana appears to double the crash compared to drivers without marijuana in their systems. [Sources: Sewell (2012), Yale University, Review of Literature; Li (2011), Meta-Analysis, Columbia University].

In summary, it is clear that marijuana impairs driving performance from laboratory and simulator studies. The research is mixed, however, concerning the effect of marijuana on crash risk. Some studies show no elevated risk while others indicate about a doubling of the risk of a crash. It is also too early to tell what the long-term effects of the legalization of marijuana will be on public health.

James C. Fell, Principal Research Scientist, NORC at the University of Chicago.
